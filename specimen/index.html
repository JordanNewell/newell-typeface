06 — Construction

Three Primitives

Every glyph is built from three primitives: vertical rails, horizontal rails, and 45° diagonals. No curves. No rounded terminals. No angles other than 45°.

The result reads as “engineered” rather than “drawn” — closer to a barcode or a circuit diagram than to a humanist serif. Strokes are monoline: every primitive carries the same 110-unit weight on a 1000-unit em. Cap height is 700, x-height 500, descender −200.

The signature gesture, inherited from the wordmark, is the N diagonal: a 45° stroke that descends from the top of one vertical rail to the bottom of another. It recurs in M, K, R, V, W, X, Y, Z and the digits 1, 2, 4, 7. That recurring diagonal is the family resemblance.

Rail (vertical)

A bar of constant width. Joins horizontals and diagonals at squared junctions.

Rail (horizontal)

Crossbars, arms, and frames. Same 110-unit thickness as every other stroke.

Diagonal (45°)

Exactly 45°. Horizontal terminal edges join cleanly with vertical rails.

07 — Honest v0.1

Known Limitations

v0.1 is functional and usable but unpolished. The DNA forbids curves, rounded terminals, and any angle other than 45° — this makes several traditional letterforms impossible to render cleanly. Known issues:

  • S reads as Z. Pure rails + 45° vocabulary cannot render an unambiguous S. v0.2 will add a third primitive scoped to S/s/2/3/5/8/$ (per ROADMAP).
  • C, D, G, O, P, Q, R, B are rectilinear approximations of curved letters — squared frames rather than curved bowls.
  • A, V, W are wider than the alphabet average because each leg is a full-cap-height 45° diagonal.
  • M, W inner notches reach only mid-height, not baseline. A baseline-reaching notch would require non-45° angles.
  • 2, 5 inherit the diagonal-spine construction; 3, 8 are stacked bowls that read as chunky frames. 0 is visually identical to O and D.
  • $, @ are particularly hard in this vocabulary. Both are best-effort v0.1 approximations.
  • Curly quotes lose their open/closed distinction — both render as small squares at cap height.

Newell is recommended for display and headline use, not long-form body text.
